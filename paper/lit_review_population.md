# Literature review (extension): how shared communication systems emerge and spread through populations

Complements `lit_review.md` (de Ruiter/Stolk/Blokpoel TCG work, Garrod & Doherty 1994, Centola & Baronchelli 2015, Hawkins, Boyce, Galantucci, Selten & Warglien, Scott-Phillips, Kirby 2008, LLM simulations are covered there and not repeated here). BibTeX: `refs_population.bib`. All entries were checked against Crossref (DOIs resolve), and against PubMed/Semantic Scholar abstracts where noted. Verified 2026-11-12.

## 1. Communities vs. isolated pairs in graphical communication (Fay, Garrod and colleagues)

**What they found.** In "Pictionary"-style drawing tasks, Garrod et al. (2007) [garrod2007] showed that signs turn from iconic to symbolic, simpler drawings only when partners interact and give feedback; repeating a sign alone does not do it. Outsiders who did not take part in the interaction also have trouble reading the signs that result (abstract, Exp. 3). Fay et al. (2010) [fay2010] compared 8-person communities, where each player drew with seven partners in turn, against isolated pairs. Pairwise interactions built a shared sign system "bottom up" across the whole community, and that global system ended up as effective and efficient as the local systems of isolated pairs. Different communities settled on different systems, which points to social collaboration rather than iterated learning. Fay et al. (2008) [fay2008] found that community-evolved signs are easier to learn and quicker to decode, and that naive overseers identify them better than pair-developed signs. They attribute this to greater *residual iconicity* (signs that stay self-explanatory), not to better encoding or detection. Their proposed mechanism: pairwise interaction produces many competing variants, and the need to align globally applies selection pressure among them. Tamariz et al. (2014) [tamariz2014] modelled how variants spread in four 8-person micro-societies. The best fit combined an *egocentric bias* (players keep their own variant) with a *content bias* (they switch when they meet a better variant), and it outperformed neutral drift. Fay et al. (2018) [fay2018] separated the parts of interaction. Behaviour alignment mainly drove effectiveness, partner feedback mainly drove efficiency, and breaking a referential precedent removed the gains. Healey et al. (2007) [healey2007] showed that *mutual modifiability* (being able to edit each other's contributions) constrains the form that representations take.

**Relation to our findings.** Our design matches the community condition of Fay et al. in a constrained, non-iconic medium. The two key results line up. After a switch, success rises mostly because Senders produce signals that are *legible to anyone*, which resembles the residual-iconicity / outsider-legibility advantage of community signs [fay2008; garrod2007]. What we do not see is Fay et al.'s endpoint, a single community-wide sign system. A likely reason is that three phases with rotating partners are shorter than Fay et al.'s full round-robin (7 partners). The Tamariz et al. selection model (egocentric plus content bias) gives a concrete hypothesis for how our within-pair style convergence could later spread across the group.

## 2. Community size and network structure

**What they found.** In a cross-linguistic analysis of more than 2,000 languages, Lupyan & Dale (2010) [lupyan2010] found that languages with more speakers, wider geographic spread and more contact have simpler inflectional morphology and rely more on lexical strategies (the Linguistic Niche Hypothesis). Wray & Grace (2007) [wray2007] contrast *esoteric* communication (within a group, among people who share context), which tolerates opaque, irregular forms, with *exoteric* communication (with strangers), which favours transparent, regular, compositional forms. Raviv, Meyer & Lev-Ari tested these ideas in the lab with micro-societies inventing a language. Larger groups (8 vs. 4) developed more systematic languages, faster and more consistently, and small groups drifted more [raviv2019]. Compositional structure emerged through interaction within a community, without generational transmission [raviv2019cog]. Network topology (fully connected vs. small-world vs. scale-free) had *no* effect on structure, success, stability or convergence. Small-world networks varied most, so topology mainly changes susceptibility to drift [raviv2020]. Lev-Ari (2018) [levari2018] found that people with smaller social networks have more malleable linguistic representations, and in simulations innovations spread faster when innovators connected to such people.

**Relation to our findings.** Our 16-player rotation is effectively "talking to strangers". The exoteric account predicts what we observe: rotation pushes Senders toward transparent, partner-independent signals rather than private pair codes. The Raviv results warn that convergence across the whole group takes many rounds and can be noisy in small or sparse samples, which fits the absence of a group-wide style after three phases.

## 3. Naming games and convention formation: models and experiments

**What they found.** Steels (1995) [steels1995] introduced language games in which agents with no central control self-organise a shared vocabulary through local interactions. Steels (2011) [steels2011] reviews this programme. Baronchelli et al. (2006) [baronchelli2006] analysed the minimal Naming Game. Under homogeneous mixing, a population passes through a disorder phase with many competing synonyms and then reaches consensus through a sharp, S-shaped transition that grows sharper as the population grows. Baronchelli (2018) [baronchelli2018] reviews models of how consensus emerges. In economics, Young (1993, 1996, 2015) [young1993; young1996; young2015] models conventions as equilibria reached through adaptive play with limited memory and noise. Under that model, local conventions can persist for long periods before tipping. Experiments with online populations bear this out. Committed minorities of roughly 25% can overturn an established naming convention [centola2018]. Large populations converge on similar category systems across independent trials, while individuals and small groups diverge [guilbeault2021].

**Relation to our findings.** Naming-game dynamics predict that a long plateau of competing variants comes *before* a sudden global convergence. Our result, "pairs converge but no group-wide convention after three phases, with a weak hint that new partners arrive pre-aligned", reads naturally as the pre-transition regime rather than as evidence against eventual convergence. The phase count is therefore a key parameter for future runs.

## 4. Signalling games

**What they found.** Lewis (1969) [lewis1969] defined a convention as a self-sustaining regularity that solves a recurrent coordination problem, and formalised the sender-receiver signalling game in which arbitrary signals gain meaning at equilibrium. Skyrms (2010) [skyrms2010] showed that such signalling systems can emerge without prior agreement, through simple reinforcement learning or evolutionary dynamics. He also showed that "partial pooling" states, where some meanings share a signal and coordination is imperfect, can trap populations.

**Relation to our findings.** The TCG is a Lewis signalling game with a structured signal space. In Lewis/Skyrms terms, our sender-driven improvement means that the *sender strategy* moves toward a separating map that any receiver can decode. A jointly learned arbitrary code would instead be a pair-specific equilibrium. The partial-pooling result offers one account of stalled convergence across the group.

## 5. Cultural evolution of language and pair-level mechanisms

**What they found.** Christiansen & Chater (2008) [christiansen2008] argue that language is shaped by cultural evolution to fit the learning and processing biases of its users, rather than the brain being adapted to language. Kirby, Tamariz, Cornish & Smith (2015) [kirby2015] show with models and experiments that linguistic structure comes from a trade-off between compressibility (pressure from learning) and expressivity (pressure from communication). Smith & Wonnacott (2010) [smith2010] show that unpredictable variation is regularised as it is transmitted across learners. At the pair level, Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs (1986) [clark1986] show that referring is collaborative: partners jointly shorten and stabilise descriptions over repeated reference. Brennan & Clark (1996) [brennan1996] show that partners form *conceptual pacts* that are partner-specific and persist even when they are overspecified. Clark (1996) [clark1996] places these results within a general account of common ground, and Tomasello (2008) [tomasello2008] grounds human communication in shared intentionality. Pickering & Garrod (2004) [pickering2004] propose *interactive alignment*: automatic priming aligns representations at every level within a dialogue, and alignment can spread from local to global.

**Relation to our findings.** Our within-pair convergence of style is the TCG counterpart of conceptual pacts and interactive alignment. The finding that the gains are *not* pair-specific (decoding by a private shared code) suggests that, under rotation, the pressure for expressivity (legibility to anyone) outweighs the partner-specific pacts that Brennan & Clark observed with fixed partners.

## 6. Encoding vs. decoding: who adapts, speaker or listener?

**What they found.** The audience-design tradition holds that speakers tailor utterances to their addressee's knowledge [clark1982]. Speakers do so imperfectly: adjustments depend on memory and on whether the speaker has the relevant experience [horton2002]. Listeners often first interpret from their own perspective and correct only later [keysar1998]. Comprehenders, however, also expect precedents [barr2002] and are slowed when a *familiar partner* breaks an established pact but not when a new partner does [metzing2003]. Brennan & Hanna (2009) [brennan2009] review the evidence that partner-specific adaptation happens on both sides. In emerging graphical systems, Fay et al. (2008) [fay2008] put the advantage of community signs in the signs' own residual iconicity rather than in better encoding or detection by users. Fay et al. (2018) [fay2018] separate a production-side contribution (behaviour alignment, which drives effectiveness) from a feedback-side contribution (which drives efficiency). Tamariz et al. (2014) [tamariz2014] model spread as producers adopting better variants (content bias). **Gap:** we found no study that directly decomposes success in an *emerging* communication system into sender-encoding and receiver-decoding components, or that tests whether innovations spread through senders or through receivers. The closest work is the partner-specificity literature and the related-work items above (Hawkins et al. on generalisation across partners, covered in `lit_review.md`).

**Relation to our findings.** Our sender/receiver decomposition speaks directly to this gap. The rise after the switch is carried mainly by Senders becoming more universally legible, not by Receivers adapting, which is consistent with both the audience-design view and with Fay's point that community signs carry their meaning in the sign itself. This asymmetry may be our most distinctive contribution relative to the graphical-communication literature.
