# Literature review: the Tacit Communication Game and related work

*Draft for the TCG-ladder write-up. Every reference was checked against Crossref/OpenAlex, the publisher page, or the proceedings page (November 2026). DOIs are given in the reference list. Details that come from a secondary description rather than the primary text are marked **[secondary]**.*

## 1. Origin and design of the Tacit Communication Game

The Tacit Communication Game (TCG) was developed at the Donders Institute and the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics in Nijmegen. The aim was to study communication when no shared code exists. De Ruiter et al. (2010) describe the task. Two players share a small grid board. Only the Sender sees the goal configuration, and the only way to convey it is to move their own token before the Receiver places theirs. Since words are ruled out, Senders have to perform *recipient design* and Receivers have to perform *intention recognition*. De Ruiter et al. found that pairs communicate remarkably well under these conditions, especially when Senders get feedback. Signalling works by changing *how* an instrumental movement is made: parts of the movement that do nothing for the Sender's own goal carry the communicative intent.

Newman-Norlund et al. (2009) showed that Senders adjust their signals to whom they believe the partner is. Senders who were told the Receiver was a child paused longer on the Receiver's goal location, and this effect faded as they tuned to the actual partner **[secondary: as summarised by Blokpoel et al., 2012]**. Noordzij et al. (2009) ran an fMRI version. Planning a communicative action (Sender) and recognising one (Receiver) engaged the same region, the right posterior superior temporal sulcus. Activity there tracked the ambiguity of the message rather than the complexity of the movement, which points to a communicative system separate from sensorimotor and linguistic processing.

*Relation to our study:* our 3×3 Sender/Receiver rebuild keeps the defining features of the original task: no shared code, signals carried by the manner of movement, and outcome feedback. The phase-1 behaviour we see (low initial success, signals built from "extra" movement) should therefore be read against these baselines.

## 2. Conceptual alignment and the emergence of meaning

Stolk and colleagues used the TCG to ask how shared meanings are negotiated. In an MEG study of live interaction, Stolk et al. (2013) found that solving novel communicative problems produced similar spectral changes in Senders and Receivers. These changes were localised to the right temporal lobe and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and they appeared before a specific problem was even presented. Stolk et al. (2014) recorded both partners at once and found cerebral coherence in the right temporal lobes. The coherence appeared only in pairs with a shared communicative history and on timescales independent of individual signals. They argue that meaning rests on shared conceptualisations ("conceptual pacts") rather than on signal-by-signal priming. Further TCG studies support the same account: rTMS over the posterior superior temporal sulcus disrupted comprehension of communicative actions (Stolk et al., 2014b), and patients with ventromedial prefrontal lesions made altered communicative decisions (Stolk et al., 2015). Stolk, Verhagen and Toni (2016) bring this work together in the *conceptual alignment* framework. On this view, communicators continuously update a model of what a signal means *to this partner*, based on their shared interaction history.

*Relation to our study:* conceptual alignment predicts that understanding is tied to a pair's own history. That fits our dip in phase 2, when partners change. It also fits our phase-3 recovery being partly a matter of reunions with earlier partners (see the partner-schedule confound in `PROJECT_NOTES.md`).

## 3. Computational accounts of TCG behaviour

Van Rooij et al. (2011) used complexity theory to analyse intentional communication framed as Bayesian inverse planning in TCG-like settings. Recipient design and intention recognition are intractable in the general case. They become tractable only under specific constraints, which raises the question of what keeps human communication fast. Blokpoel et al. (2012) fitted trial-by-trial TCG data to compare simple cue-based heuristics (for example "anchor and adjust") with explicit perspective taking. They concluded that the specificity of Senders' adjustments is better explained by perspective taking. Blokpoel et al. (2013) give a related computational-level account of how quickly goals are inferred from observed actions.

*Relation to our study:* these models treat the Sender as the locus of design, which fits our finding that phase-3 gains come mainly from clearer Sender encoding. Our drift-style models of individual signalling sit closer to the heuristic end of this debate.

## 4. Strategies, individual differences, autism and development

The best-documented Sender strategies split a movement into a *location* part and an *orientation* part. The Sender's token pauses relatively longer on the Receiver's goal tile. Orientation is then conveyed by a "wiggle", a possibly repeated move from the goal tile to an adjacent tile and back. Blokpoel et al. (2012) report the wiggle as the most common strategy **[secondary detail; see their Methods]**. Volman, Noordzij and Toni (2012) sorted strategies on novel problems into COARSE ones, which carry little orientation information, and REFINED ones, which show rotation explicitly. Pairs reached about 49% accuracy on new problems and 95% on familiar ones. No single psychometric measure dominated success. Receiver fluid intelligence predicted performance and learning rate. Mismatch between partners on some traits made REFINED strategies less likely.

Studies of autism separate signal *production* from *alignment*. Wadge et al. (2019) found that autistic adults matched neurotypical adults in generating and adjusting intelligible signals. They struggled, however, when a signal's meaning depended on recent communicative history. Koch et al. (2026) report that autistic participants kept stereotype-driven, partner-dependent adjustments without adapting dynamically, whereas non-autistic participants adapted over the interaction. In development, Stolk et al. (2013b) showed that five-year-olds spontaneously organised non-verbal signals around their beliefs about the addressee. The size of these adjustments was predicted by time spent in daycare.

*Relation to our study:* our single-turn embeddings recover the same kinds of dimension, such as dwell on the goal and orientation marking. Like Volman et al., we find Sender clarity is one main driver of success. Unlike them, our tournament design isolates encoding changes from Receiver changes.

## 5. Conventions in pairs versus populations

The TCG belongs to a wider *experimental semiotics* literature (Galantucci & Garrod, 2011). In Galantucci's (2005) task, pairs built communication systems from scratch through a novel graphical channel. In Scott-Phillips, Kirby and Ritchie's (2009) Embodied Communication Game, players have to "signal signalhood" using movement alone, which makes it a close cousin of the TCG. Selten and Warglien (2007) showed that simple and sometimes compositional codes emerge in coordination games with costly symbols. Kirby, Cornish and Smith (2008) showed that structure accumulates when a language is transmitted across generations.

Whether a convention spreads depends on who interacts with whom. In Garrod and Doherty's (1994) maze-game study, communities whose members rotated partners eventually converged on more stable shared conventions than isolated pairs. Centola and Baronchelli (2015) found that global naming conventions emerged spontaneously in homogeneously mixing networks but not in spatially structured ones. Hawkins, Frank and Goodman (2020) documented idiosyncratic, pair-specific conventions in repeated reference games. Hawkins et al. (2023) formalise how partner-specific common ground is learned quickly within a dyad and gradually abstracted into community-wide priors over many partners. Boyce et al. (2024) show that larger groups and "thin" interaction channels hamper convergence on group conventions (see also Hawkins, Goodman & Goldstone, 2019).

*Relation to our study:* our pattern matches the early, dyad-dominated regime of these models. Partners pull each other's style together, but with only three phases and a few partners there is too little mixing to abstract a population prior, so no group-wide convention forms. Centola and Baronchelli's network result predicts this for sparse rotation schedules.

## 6. LLMs as simulators of participants, and similarity-based embeddings

LLMs are increasingly used as stand-ins for human participants. Argyle et al. (2023) conditioned GPT-3 on demographic backstories to produce "silicon samples" that reproduced subgroup response patterns. Aher, Arriaga and Kalai (2023) proposed "Turing Experiments" that replicate classic studies, and they reported a "hyper-accuracy distortion" in newer models. Park et al. (2023) built generative agents with memory and reflection that produce believable social behaviour. Binz et al. (2025) fine-tuned an LLM on Psych-101, trial-level data from over 60,000 participants in 160 experiments. The resulting model, Centaur, predicted held-out participants better than domain-specific cognitive models. There are several critiques. Dillion et al. (2023) set out when LLMs could and could not replace participants. Wang, Morgenstern and Dickerson (2025) show that LLM stand-ins misportray and flatten identity groups. Liu and Ding (2025, preprint) question whether Centaur's success reflects understanding of task instructions.

Our turn embeddings build on the triplet odd-one-out approach. Hebart et al. (2020) derived interpretable, sparse object dimensions from millions of human odd-one-out judgments (THINGS), and Muttenthaler et al. (2022) provided a variational method for such embeddings (VICE). Similarity judgments from LLMs recover human-like structure: Marjieh et al. (2024) did this across six sensory modalities, and Du et al. (2025) did it for object concepts using LLM triplet judgments.

*Relation to our study:* we use an LLM in both roles, as similarity judge and as player simulator. The critiques apply directly. Our validity checks find that LLM-predicted Sender turns change less than real players' turns do, in line with the "too clean / hyper-accurate" distortion that Aher et al. describe.
